## Abstract

Electric utilities study generation and load interconnection requests in
**separate** tracks, and US regulators have now begun requiring the opposite:
the Department of Energy's October 2025 Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking
and FERC's June 2026 Section 206 show-cause orders both name co-located load
and generation as a required reform area. Neither action was accompanied by a
measure of what the separation being undone costs. We supply one.

We define the **residual** — `JOINT − max(sequential study orders)` — as
distribution-feeder hosting capacity that is only jointly feasible, and that
therefore no segregated study process recovers however it orders its queues.
Across four independently sourced feeder families — two EPRI utility-scale
circuits, two UK LV feeders, a US split-phase feeder run through a second
power-flow engine, and a published 55-node 21 kV Swiss MV feeder — residuals
range from zero to 76% of the joint total, measured with an AC-verified
Generalized-LinDistFlow instrument that certifies a feasible lower bound rather
than a relaxation. The residual is nonzero where the active physical constraint
makes generation and load complements and zero where only one sign can worsen
it, and we demonstrate the regime crossing within a single feeder as its queue
oversubscribes.

We then test whether that regime can be called *before* the residual is
computed, from single-signed studies alone, and report a **negative result**:
two feeders with identical binding patterns have opposite residuals, so no such
blind test separates them. What does predict the residual in advance are two
measurable properties of the pending queue — oversubscription, which gates it,
and request granularity, which sets its size — and these carry the paper's
actionable recommendation.

Separately, replaying a certified accept-set against a feeder's own measured
diurnal profile shows every snapshot magnitude to be fragile: feasible in 49 of
192 real intervals, and moved to 233% line loading by an ordinary cloud
transient 45 minutes from a feasible one. We therefore report all magnitudes as
snapshot magnitudes, and rest the contribution on the process-design conclusion
rather than on any capacity number.

## 1. Introduction

Every interconnection request a distribution utility receives — a PV array, a
battery, an EV-charging depot, a data center — is, by convention, assigned to
one of two study tracks by its sign: generation or load. The tracks run on
different tariffs, often in different departments, and each studies its own
queue against a feeder whose *other* queue it typically does not see. This
separation long predates any reform debate; it is simply how connection
processes are organized. The question this paper asks is what that
organizational fact costs in hosting capacity, independent of which
optimization method either track happens to use.

**The reform that makes this timely.** On 23 October 2025 the US Department of
Energy directed FERC, via an [Advance Notice of Proposed
Rulemaking](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2025-10/403%20Large%20Loads%20Letter.pdf)
(Docket [RM26-4](https://www.ferc.gov/rm26-4)), to let customers file joint,
co-located load-and-generation interconnection requests, with final action
requested by April 2026. On 18 June 2026 FERC issued [Section 206 show-cause
orders](https://www.ferc.gov/news-events/news/ferc-launches-aggressive-targeted-action-speed-large-load-integration)
to PJM, MISO, SPP, CAISO, ISO-NE and NYISO (Docket Nos. EL26-67 through
EL26-72), naming "accommodating co-location arrangements and behind-the-meter
generation" as one of five required reform areas. Regulators are moving from
separate to joint study without a measure of what separation costs. That
measure is this paper.

**What is established, and what is not.** That adding load raises PV hosting
capacity, and that adding PV relieves a load-limited feeder, is net-load
bookkeeping: demand response improving hosting capacity is a reviewed
literature (Hwang et al. 2023), smart-charged EV load raising combined PV–EV
hosting capacity is measured (Fachrizal et al. 2021), and active network
management raising DG hosting capacity is established (Capitanescu et al.
2015). We are *not* claiming to have discovered that generation and load are
complementary. Our contribution is institutional and combinatorial:

1. We cost the **segregated study process** — the parallel and sequential study
   tracks a utility actually runs — as an artifact of process design in its own
   right, rather than optimizing a single co-study and reporting its size
   (Sections 3.1 and 5.1).
2. We give a **mechanism** for when that cost is irreducible, grounded in which
   physical constraint binds (Section 3.2); we then test whether that mechanism
   can be evaluated blind, from single-signed studies alone, and report that on
   our corpus it cannot (Section 5.2). We tie the irreducibility to the
   breakdown of the independence-system structure that justifies serial,
   first-come-first-served processing in the first place: a mixed
   generation/load queue is a gross-substitutes violation (Section 3.3).
3. We measure the **temporal validity** of the mechanism by replaying a
   certified accept-set against a feeder's own measured diurnal profile, and
   scope our magnitude claims to what survives it (Section 5.6).

The first two are process and combinatorial claims built on established
physics, not new physical findings. Section 2 places this against the closest
prior art: a joint-capacity co-optimization literature that evaluates the joint
number but does not decompose the segregated-process cost, and a
transmission-level industry report that argues the same qualitative point
without distribution AC power flow or a predictive rule.

## 2. Related work

**The physics we build on, not contest.** Capitanescu et al. (2015) show
network reconfiguration raises DG hosting capacity under active management.
Fachrizal et al. (2021) show smart EV charging and PV curtailment raise
combined PV–EV hosting capacity on an LV feeder. Hwang et al. (2023) review
demand response's hosting-capacity benefit. Minimum load being the worst case
for PV hosting capacity is textbook.

**The carve-out from joint-capacity co-optimization.** Chen et al. (2017)
evaluate PV and EV-charging capacity *jointly*, under a distributionally-robust
chance constraint — the same move the DG–flexible-load–storage co-optimization
strand makes more generally. This is the literature a skim reviewer will reach
for first, and the distinction matters: prior co-optimization work compares a
joint optimum against a single separate baseline and reports the joint number
is larger; none decomposes that gap into the part any study *order* recovers
versus the irreducible residual no order does, and none predicts which case
obtains from the active constraint. The joint number is the *premise* of that
literature. The process cost of not being allowed to compute it, and its
combinatorial signature, are ours.

**The closest policy-adjacent precedent.** The Brattle Group (2025, for Bloom
Energy) argues that studying co-located load+generation — "Energy Parks," under
a control mechanism capping net grid exchange — jointly avoids upgrades a
separate load-only study would trigger: the same intuition as our residual, in
the same policy context. It is a transmission-level, N-1-deliverability
consulting report: no distribution AC power flow, no combinatorial mechanism,
no predictive rule, and no residual decomposition. We cite it as the industry
precedent for the question and supply the distribution-level physics, the
mechanism, and the rule.

**Queue-reform economics.** Pollitt et al. (2026) frame primary
connection-rights allocation as a knapsack problem and fault
first-come-first-served (FCFS) for ignoring project feasibility, progress and
value; Eurelectric (2025) makes the industry case for moving distribution
queues off FCFS. De Santi et al. (2025, doi:10.1016/j.apenergy.2025.126260)
carry that question into a distribution power-flow model, evaluating flexible
connection agreements and curtailment rules on a synthetic urban European
network under an AC optimal power flow, and find an order effect *within* a
single track: individually negotiated flexible capacity is absorbed by
early-assessed generators, leaving less network capacity for positions later in
the queue. Their queue is entirely generation, which is where this paper starts
rather than where it ends. The residual of Section 3.1 measures what a second,
opposite-signed track can no longer recover, and it is zero by construction in
a single-signed queue however that queue is ordered or curtailed.
Section 3.3 extends this literature with the physical
feasibility system underneath the knapsack: a mixed generation/load queue is
not even an independence system, so the "value" a reform must rank is jointly
determined, not additive across projects.

**Feeder data.** The Swiss feeder in Section 4.2 is EPFL's Distributed
Electrical Systems Laboratory's published 55-node, 21 kV distribution grid (Yi
et al., *Applied Energy* 326, 2022, 119942), used with the authors' permission.

## 3. When study order matters, and when it cannot be made to

### 3.1 Three ways to study one queue, and the residual between them

Take one fixed set of pending generation and load interconnection requests on
one feeder. There are three ways a utility can study it:

- **Segregated–parallel.** Certify the generation requests against the baseline
  feeder; certify the load requests against the baseline feeder, independently;
  connect the union of what each track approved. Each track is blind to the
  other. This is the worst case for segregation — an upper bound on what it
  costs, not the process a utility actually runs.
- **Segregated–sequential.** The realistic process: study one queue, approve
  it, add it to the base case, then study the other queue against
  baseline-plus-the-first. The second track gets to exploit whatever the first
  track's approvals did to the feeder's voltage or loading. We run both orders
  (generation-first and load-first).
- **Joint.** Certify all requests together.

Figure 1 shows all four outcomes measured on a single Ckt7 cell. The quantity
this paper holds itself to is the **residual**:

```
residual = JOINT − max(sequential order₁, sequential order₂)
```

the hosting capacity *no* study order recovers, because it comes from requests
that are only jointly feasible — a PV request that needs a co-located load to
be seatable never clears a generation-first track, and vice versa. We quote it
as a fraction of the joint total, `(JOINT − max_order)/JOINT`, bounded in
[0,1], plus the absolute kW where a feeder's magnitude is informative. We do
not quote it as a percentage over the segregated baseline, which explodes
whenever that baseline is near zero for structural reasons unrelated to the
physics of interest.

![The four study outcomes and the two gaps between them](figures/fig1_definition.png)

*Figure 1. The four study outcomes and the two gaps between them, on one
illustrative EPRI Ckt7 cell: K=17 monodisperse 100 kW requests (9 generation +
8 load) at voltage setpoint 1.04 p.u., seed 0. A segregated parallel study
certifies 700 kW; running the two tracks in sequence recovers most of the gap

neither order. That remainder is the residual. Every bar is a multiple of 100
kW because the queue is monodisperse at that size, and the parallel bar is the
exact sum of the two solo bars by construction; neither is a finding.*

The residual is a **floor** on the realized loss, not the realized loss itself:
`max_order` is optimistic, since a utility does not choose the
capacity-maximizing order — the order is set by which request happened to be
ready first. Where recovery *requires* a specific order, an
institutionally-determined order that gets it wrong realizes the full parallel
gap even where the residual itself is zero. The residual is therefore the
irreducible floor under the best order; the realized loss under whatever order
institutions actually impose lies between it and the parallel gap.

### 3.2 The invariant: does the active constraint admit both signs?

Whether the residual is zero or large is decided by one property of the binding
constraint.

A voltage band is two-sided — an upper and a lower limit — so generation
(pushing voltage up) and load (pulling it down) are aggressors on opposite
sides almost automatically; voltage binding is essentially always two-way. A
thermal or ampacity rating `|I| ≤ I_max` is one-sided, so it is two-way only
when the binding element carries reversible power flow — a shared line or
transformer that a generation-heavy accept-set loads in one direction and a
load-heavy accept-set loads in the other. Where the binding thermal element
sits upstream of every request (a substation transformer that only export can
relieve), the dependency is one-way: load needs generation's relief, generation
needs nothing back, and a generation-first sequential study recovers the entire
joint capacity. Where the binding element is a lateral or spur carrying
genuinely reversible flow, generation and load are each independent aggressors
that cancel only together, and no order recovers them.

The invariant is therefore: **does the active constraint admit both generation
and load as independent aggressors?** It is a property of which constraint
binds, not of the feeder — so the same feeder can sit on either side of it as
its queue changes (Section 5.4), and a constraint's type alone ("thermal" or
"voltage") does not settle it.

### 3.3 Why a mixed queue breaks the guarantee behind serial processing

First-come-first-served processing is not merely a fairness convention; on a
single-signed queue it carries a capacity guarantee. A queue of all generation,
or all load, is — to the capacity objective — an **independence system**: any
feasible subset's subsets are feasible, downward closure holds, and greedy
processing in any order returns a maximal accept-set. Where requests are
additionally equal-sized, the system is a **uniform matroid** and greedy is
optimal in total capacity in every order. The correct governing bound on
greedy's loss in the general independence-system case is the rank quotient
(Jenkyns 1976; Korte & Hausmann 1978): greedy returns a maximal, not maximum,
set, and the two coincide only on a matroid.

A mixed generation/load queue has none of this structure, and the reason is the
same complementarity that creates the residual. The right frame is gross
substitutes (Kelso & Crawford 1982) / M♮-concavity (Murota 2003): the
substitutes condition is precisely the boundary at which matroid structure and
greedy optimality hold, and **generation and load being complements is the
violation of gross substitutes**. That is the one-sentence reason every
FCFS/greedy capacity guarantee dies exactly as real queues acquire both signs —
not because a particular optimizer is weak. A parallel line of work on
greedoids (Korte, Lovász & Schrader 1991), matroid secretary problems (Babaioff
et al. 2007; Feldman et al. 2018), and random-serial dictatorship's ex-post
efficiency on single-unit assignment (Abdulkadiroğlu & Sönmez 1998; Bogomolnaia
& Moulin 2001) confirms why these guarantees do not transfer to a mixed queue,
rather than offering a positive substitute for them. Section 5.5 tests the
prediction directly.

## 4. Method

### 4.1 A certified feasible lower bound, not a relaxation

Every certification in this paper — joint, sequential, or the request sizing
that produces them — uses a Generalized LinDistFlow (GLDF; Huang, Cui, Zhou &
Bernstein, IEEE CDC 2021, doi:10.1109/CDC45484.2021.9682997) linear power-flow
surrogate as a search instrument, re-linearized at the current incumbent
solution and iterated
to a fixed point, with every iterate re-verified by an exact AC power flow
(`pandapower.runpp` or OpenDSS, matched to the feeder's native format). Unlike
classic Baran–Wu LinDistFlow, the generalized form is built directly from the
network's bus-admittance matrix and represents meshed topology, shunt
capacitors, and line charging natively — needed for one of our feeders, where
they account for 63.8% of reactive load. Re-linearizing at the incumbent rather
than forever at the empty feeder corrects for the model's sensitivities being
otherwise anchored at the wrong operating point; iterating to a fixed point
converges the anchor to the solution itself.

The result is a certified, AC-feasible allocation at every reported number — a
lower bound on true achievable capacity, not an estimate that could turn out
infeasible. GLDF is a linear *approximation*, not a convex *relaxation*: its
feasible region is not provably a superset of the true one, so "optimal under
GLDF" is not a proof about the true AC optimum. The AC-reverification step is
what makes every reported allocation trustworthy regardless.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 The residual across the corpus

The table below and Figure 2 report the residual across the corpus, together
with the regime we assign each cell. Every cell we classify as admitting only
one sign has a residual of exactly zero; every nonzero residual comes from a
cell we classify as admitting both. That holds across two power-flow engines,
four network families, three regulatory standards, and both signs of voltage
violation (SMART-DS is undervoltage-limited, every other family overvoltage- or
thermal-limited).

Two qualifications belong here rather than in a footnote. First, the regime is
a property of a *cell*, not of an individual draw: a two-way cell can still
show a zero residual in a particular draw where no complementary pair happens
to land close enough electrically to help, which is why the Ckt24
oversubscribed row reports a count of positive draws rather than a median. The
classification predicts that a residual is *possible*, not that every draw
realizes one. Second, and more seriously, the classification in column three is
**ours**: we assigned it by inspecting which constraint binds on each feeder,
and we did so knowing the residuals. Section 5.2 asks whether it can be
computed without that knowledge, and reports that on this corpus it cannot.

### 5.2 Can the regime be called before the residual is computed?

An invariant whose antecedent is only ever evaluated after seeing the
consequent is not a rule but a restatement, and nothing can refute it. Since
"the active constraint admits both signs as independent aggressors" is
uncomfortably close to "some requests are only jointly feasible" — which is
what the residual measures — we made the classification mechanical and ran it
blind (`experiments/106_regime_classifier.py`).

**Two algorithms, both consuming only single-signed studies.** Both start by
certifying the generation-only queue and the load-only queue against the
baseline feeder, and then, for every request a track could not seat, recording
which constraints blocked it at that track's own optimum. Constraint identity
is the two-sided voltage band as one object (generation pinning the upper limit
and load pinning the lower are aggressors on the same band) and each thermal
element separately, since the paper's two-way thermal case is a *shared* line
or transformer.

- **Strict.** Two-way iff both tracks drive the *same* constraint to its limit.
  The literal reading of "both signs are independent aggressors".
- **Relief.** Two-way iff, for each track, some single request of the opposite
  sign opens slack on a constraint blocking it. The weaker and arguably more
  faithful reading, since what makes a request jointly feasible is that the
  other sign *relieves* its binding constraint, not that the other sign could
  violate that constraint from the far side.

Neither algorithm receives, computes, or imports a joint or sequential result.
Each cell's prediction was emitted first and the measured residual looked up
afterwards.

**Result: neither predicts the residual on this corpus.** Over 8 cells × 3
seeds, the strict test agrees on 15/24 cell-draws (5 of 8 cells), missing all
three EPRI cells that do have a residual. The relief test agrees on 21/24 (7 of
8 cells) — but it returns "two-way" for *every* cell in the corpus, so it has
no demonstrated discriminating power; its one error is a false positive on the
cell where the residual is identically zero.

The decisive observation is a pair. **EPRI Ckt7 and lightly-loaded EPRI Ckt24
receive identical verdicts from both algorithms** (strict: one-way; relief:
two-way), **and their residuals are opposite**: positive in 8 of 9 Ckt7 cells,
exactly zero in 9 of 9 Ckt24 cells. On Ckt7 the generation track is stopped by
the voltage band and the load track by its own service transformers; on Ckt24
the generation track is stopped by voltage and a handful of service
transformers and the load track by service transformers plus the substation.
The blind data are the same shape in both cases. What differs is quantitative —
on Ckt7 a co-located load buys enough voltage headroom to seat another 100 kW
request, and the joint study seats 9 generation requests where the generation
track alone seats 4; on Ckt24 the same relief exists locally but is never worth
an additional request. No test built from which constraints bind and which are
locally relievable separates these two feeders, because the difference is not
in the binding pattern.

**What we conclude, and what we therefore do not claim.** The invariant is a
sound *description* of the three mechanisms in Section 5.3 — each of those
walk-throughs is verified directly on its feeder — and it correctly organizes
the corpus once a human has identified the binding element and reasoned about
which signs load it. What it is not, on this evidence, is a rule a utility can
evaluate from a baseline power-flow solution without doing the joint study. We
accordingly drop that claim (Section 6.1) and rest the paper's actionable
content on the two measured knobs of Section 5.4, which *are* computable in
advance.

One genuine out-of-sample test survives. The Swiss feeder was not used to
develop any of this: its regime was called ampacity-limited and one-way before
we ran it, and Prof. Mario Paolone, lead author of the network's source
publication, confirmed that regime independently from his own knowledge of the
feeder (Section 5.3). That is one prediction, on one network, confirmed by
someone who could have contradicted it — worth stating precisely because the
rest of this section shows how easily such a rule can be fitted after the fact.

### 5.3 Three mechanisms behind the three regimes

**One-way, residual 0: an upstream thermal element.** On Ckt24 lightly loaded,
the binding element is the substation transformer, upstream of every request. A
new load worsens its loading and needs generation's export to relieve it;
generation only ever relieves it and needs nothing back — a one-way dependency,
load → generation. Studying generation first recovers 100% of joint capacity in
every tested cell. The Swiss feeder at realistic queue sizes is the same case
on different hardware: its shared upstream lines bind before anything else, and
Prof. Mario Paolone (EPFL DESL), lead author of the network's source
publication, confirmed the regime independently from his own knowledge of the
feeder ("for this specific feeder, ampacity limits are binding way before the
voltage ones"; personal communication, 2026).

**Two-way by a reversible thermal element.** Move the binding thermal element
to a lateral carrying mixed flow and the sign flips. On an ampacity-limited MV
spur to a co-located generation+load site, generation alone overloads it by
reverse flow and load alone overloads it by forward flow — each an independent
aggressor, cancelling only together (residual 75% of joint, voltage never
binding). ENWL's n2f1 supplies the real-feeder instance: at 100 kW a fifth PV
request drives a lateral to 109% loading while feeder voltage sits at only
106.7% of its overvoltage limit — a genuinely thermal, two-way constraint on
real infrastructure, not a constructed toy. The Swiss feeder reaches the same
regime once oversubscribed: at K=48 with ~500 kW requests, the joint accept-set
seats ~24 MW where no sequential order exceeds ~22.3 MW.

**Two-way by a voltage band.** The mirror-image case: PV pushes voltage up and
needs load to pull it down; load pulls it down and needs PV to push it up. On
Ckt7 the residual is positive in 8 of 9 tested cells, and the best sequential
order even flips with the request mix. ENWL n13f4 is the clean case — a fourth
PV pushes `v_max` to 1.136 p.u. while the shared line is still at 72% loading —
on an independent, non-EPRI family with a different voltage standard. SMART-DS
shows the same mechanism with the sign flipped: it is genuinely
undervoltage-limited, and its co-located pair still cancels.

### 5.4 The regime belongs to the queue, not to the feeder

Two measurable knobs, both properties of the pending queue rather than of the
network, set both the residual's size and — on Ckt24 — which constraint binds
at all. Figure 3 shows both.

**Oversubscription is the switch.** On Ckt7, holding request size at 30 kW and
sweeping the total requested, the residual is exactly 0 while the generation
track fits solo — a sequential generation-first order recovers the entire
parallel gap — and turns on the moment the track is oversubscribed: 2.6% /
12.3% / 19.3% of joint at oversubscription 1.05× / 1.30× / 1.41×. A queue
exists at all only because demand exceeds hosting capacity, so this is the
regime that matters in practice.

**Granularity is the amplifier.** At fixed oversubscription, coarser requests
raise the residual — roughly 9% of joint across 12–75 kW requests on Ckt7,
rising to 37% at 100 kW, where a single request begins to fail solo and only a
co-located load rescues it. On Ckt24 the granularity curve is a band rather
than a ramp: it peaks near 100 kW and falls back to 0 at 150 kW, where a
request is so coarse it barely seats at all and the queue is too sparse to hold
complementary pairs.

![The residual is set by the queue, not fixed by the feeder](figures/fig3_knobs.png)

*Figure 3. The residual is set by the queue, not fixed by the feeder. (a) On
Ckt7 at fixed 30 kW granularity, the residual is identically zero until the
generation track is oversubscribed, then rises monotonically with

**The same knobs move the binding constraint itself.** On Ckt24, fine 30 kW
requests stay thermal-limited (residual 0) past 4 MW requested; coarse 100 kW
requests oversubscribed 2.5× or more cross into overvoltage-limited territory,
where a residual appears in a minority of draws. In one directly verified cell,
PV alone seats 4 requests (`v_max`-pinned at 1.046 p.u., transformer below its
cap) while joint study seats 6 — the two extra requests AC-feasible, reachable
by no sequential order, a residual of 30% of the joint total. A single feeder
therefore sits on both sides of the invariant depending on what its queue looks
like, which is why the rule must be evaluated against the pending queue and not
stored as a feeder attribute.

### 5.5 Downward closure fails: the queue is not an independence system

Section 3.3 predicts that the structure justifying serial processing — downward
closure, and with it greedy's maximality guarantee — fails on a mixed queue. It
does, and the failure is directly measurable.

Dropping one accepted request at a time from 42 certified accept-sets, we find
feasibility failing in **15 of 42 cells**: a set is feasible while a subset of
it is not, because removing a load pushes the generation-heavy remainder past a
voltage limit the pair together respected. This is the independence-system
axiom failing on real distribution feeders, not a numerical artifact, and it is
the concrete content of the gross-substitutes violation. Every capacity
guarantee that first-come-first-served processing inherits from matroid
structure is void on a queue like this — not because a particular optimizer is
weak, but because the feasibility system underneath the queue is not the kind
of object those guarantees are about. A queue-reform proposal that ranks
projects by "value" (Pollitt et al. 2026; Eurelectric 2025) is ranking a
quantity that is jointly determined rather than additive across projects.

A second, independent avoidable loss — the arrival-order lottery within a fixed
study boundary — is of comparable size to the residual and compounds with it,
since re-ordering a queue and widening the study boundary are different
interventions. It is reported in [Appendix A](#appendix-a-the-arrival-order-lottery)
rather than here, because it is a property of study *order* and this paper is
about study *boundaries*.

### 5.6 Complementarity in time: what a real day does to a certified accept-set

Every result above is a snapshot certification. A residual that depends on
generation cancelling load is only as robust as the simultaneity between them,
so we tested that simultaneity directly, on the two feeders whose data permit
it.

**Two temporal extremes (SMART-DS).** The joint-certified, co-located SMART-DS
accept-set was re-solved by exact AC power flow at the two off-diagonal corners
of the (PV, load) box: midday (PV at nameplate, load off) and evening (load at
nameplate, PV off). It violates the voltage band 6/6 at each corner — `v_max`
reaches 1.38–1.53 at midday, `v_min` falls to 0.65–0.79 in the evening. The
pair cancels only when both are simultaneously present.

**A real measured day (Swiss feeder).** The Swiss feeder's oversubscribed
residual rests on ~12 MW of accepted PV cancelling ~12 MW of accepted load on
the same shared lines; load alone supports only ~2.3 MW, so most of the
accepted load was only grid-compatible because the PV was simultaneously
present. This is the only feeder in our corpus shipping a genuine measured
diurnal profile — 96 intervals across two day-types for both load and
irradiance — so we re-solved AC feasibility of the exact certified accept-set
at every one of those 192 real snapshots, scaling accepted PV and load requests
by the feeder's own measured shapes.

It is feasible in 49 of 192. Before trusting this we ruled out a modeling
artifact two ways: the feeder's native load alone (zero interconnection queue)
never exceeds 60% line loading anywhere across all 768 shipped snapshots, so
the violations are not corrupted input data; and bus-index mapping is identical
across independently rebuilt network instances, so they are not a wiring bug.

![A certified accept-set replayed against the feeder's own measured day](figures/fig4_swiss_day.png)

*Figure 4. A snapshot-certified accept-set replayed against the Swiss feeder's
own measured profiles, for both shipped day-types. Top: the measured PV and
load scaling factors. Bottom: resulting maximum line loading, with the ampacity
limit dashed and AC-feasible intervals shaded. The accept-set is feasible in 49
of 192 intervals and reaches 553% loading at the midday PV peak. The feasible
intervals split into low-demand windows (overnight, where nothing is large
enough to overload) and genuine cancellation windows (late afternoon), and an
ordinary irradiance transient moves the feeder between feasible and 233%
loading within 45 minutes.*

The feasible set does not form a smooth band around the certification hour. It
fragments into two distinct kinds of window, visible in Figure 4: genuine
*cancellation* windows, where PV and load both sit near their reference scale
and offset each other, and separate *low-demand* windows, where both are simply
too small to overload anything regardless of cancellation. Between two
cancellation windows on the same day, an ordinary measured irradiance dip — an
unremarkable cloud, not a synthetic extreme — pushes the same accept-set to
233% line loading only 45 minutes from a fully feasible interval.

Two feeders, two different binding constraints, the same answer: a residual
that depends on generation cancelling load at one moment in time does not
survive a real profile in general. This is sharper evidence than the two-corner
test alone could produce — a corner check samples the day/night extremes but
not an intraday transient — and it generalizes beyond deliberately co-located
pairs, reproducing on a large, spatially-spread, oversubscription-driven
accepted set. Section 6.2 states how this scopes each magnitude class.

### 5.7 Sensitivity

**Segregation is conservative, not unsafe.** The union of the two
separately-certified parallel tracks is AC-feasible in 24/24 tested cells
across both EPRI feeders. Segregation leaves capacity on the table; it does not
endanger the feeder. Sequential study is AC-safe by construction, so the only
failure mode is the parallel one, and it did not fire.

**Random placement is a lower bound on both ends.** SMART-DS shows this
directly: on a 3,443-node feeder, randomly-placed complements land too far
apart electrically to help (residual ~0), while co-locating a generation and a
load request at the same site lifts the residual to 50% of joint. A real,
spatially-correlated queue lies between these, and the co-located end is the
one the co-location reform actually targets.

**Smart-inverter Volt/VAr does not dissolve the residual.** The standard
objection to a voltage-regime residual is that smart-inverter reactive support
removes it. Answering that in the *solo* currency (joint vs. generation-alone)
would not answer it at all, for the reason §3.1 gives: the solo comparison
overstates segregation's cost. So we re-ran the full three-way study — both
solo tracks, both sequential orders, and the joint study — with an IEEE
1547-2018 Category B Volt/VAr droop (absorbing up to 44% of `|P|` above 1.03
p.u., solved to a fixed point) enabled in *every* stage, including the first
track's already-approved inverters while the second track is studied.

| Feeder | Residual, fixed power factor | Residual, Volt/VAr throughout |
|---|---|---|
| ENWL n13f4 | 78.3% of joint | 69.6% of joint |
| ENWL n2f1 | 65.7% of joint | 65.7% of joint |

*(Medians over four seeds, K=24 = 12 generation + 12 load at 100 kW. These
cells use a greedy packer rather than the GLDF certifier of §4.1, identically
in both columns, so the with/without comparison is exact; the absolute levels
are not directly comparable with §5.1's table.)*

The residual survives, and on n2f1 it does not move at all. Two mechanisms,
both consequences of `r ≫ x` on these LV feeders, defeat reactive support:
weak voltage authority (absorbing the full 44% drops `v_max` from 1.136 to
only 1.098 p.u., because `ΔV ≈ rP + xQ` and `x` is small) and a thermal
backfire (that same reactive absorption raises line current, converting an
overvoltage limit into a thermal one). Reactive support cannot substitute for
the *real*-power complementarity that makes the residual irreducible. Two
feeders is not a sweep; extending this to every cell is future work.

**Ckt24's substation regulator does not move the number.** Un-freezing Ckt24's
tap and attaching its real line-drop-compensating LTC (verified against
OpenDSS's own solved operating point) shows the live regulator inert at every
certified optimum: it needs roughly 20 MW of aggregate export to move, while
the feeder saturates on localized end-of-lateral overvoltage at 1–3 MW — a
scale mismatch a substation-level tap cannot relieve. The frozen-tap model is
not merely defensible; it is correct to within the regulator's authority.

**Voltage-band choice does not move the Swiss result.** The Swiss residual is
identical under 0.95–1.05 p.u. and 0.90–1.10 p.u. bands, since that feeder
never approaches either — consistent with its being ampacity-limited
throughout.

## 6. What the rule decides, and what each number is scoped to

### 6.1 What the rule decides

Two things a utility can act on come out of this work, and it matters which is
which.

**What the invariant supports: a vocabulary, not a test.** The invariant
identifies the mechanism to look for. Where the binding constraint is an
element only one sign can worsen — a substation transformer that export only
relieves, as on lightly-loaded Ckt24 and on the Swiss feeder at realistic queue
sizes — a sequential, generation-first study recovered the full joint capacity
in every cell we measured. Where the two signs are genuine complements at the
binding constraint — a two-sided voltage band, or a shared element carrying
reversible flow — some capacity was only jointly feasible, no sequential order
recovered it, and the best order sometimes flipped with the request mix. This
is a correct account of what happens and why. It is not, on our evidence,
something a planner can evaluate from a baseline solve: Section 5.2 shows two
feeders with identical blind binding patterns and opposite residuals, and no
blind test we could construct separates them.

**What is computable in advance: the two knobs.** The actionable predictors are
the ones Section 5.4 measures, because both are properties of the pending queue
and neither requires the joint study:

- **Oversubscription is a gate.** The residual was identically zero on Ckt7
  while the generation track fitted solo, and turned on the moment it did not.
  A queue that fits does not need co-study.
- **Granularity sets the stakes.** At fixed oversubscription, coarse requests
  (around 100 kW on these feeders) produce the largest residuals, because a
  single coarse request is the one that fails solo and needs a co-located
  partner. Finely divided queues lose little to segregation; lumpy ones lose
  the most.

So the defensible recommendation is conditional, and cheap to evaluate:
co-study is worth its cost on oversubscribed, lumpy queues, and is close to
free to skip on queues that fit or that are finely divided. Determining the
residual's actual size still requires running the joint study.

### 6.2 The scope of every magnitude in this paper

Section 5.6 shows that a residual resting on same-instant cancellation is
fragile. One scoping rule follows, and it applies to the whole paper rather
than to a protected subset of it:

> Every capacity magnitude reported here is a **snapshot** magnitude, certified
> AC-feasible at a single representative operating point. On the one feeder in
> the corpus with a measured diurnal profile, the certified accept-set was
> feasible in 49 of 192 real intervals — it lost 74% of them. No magnitude in
> Section 5.1's table should be read as a quantity that persists across a real
> year, and none is offered as one.

We do not exempt the spatially-diffuse cells (Ckt7, Ckt24, ENWL) from this.
Their complementarity is spatial rather than same-bus, and one might argue a
feeder-wide constraint's binding status is less likely to flip on a 15-minute
transient than a single cancelling pair's — but that is an argument, not a
measurement, and it would rest on exactly the kind of plausibility this paper's
own Section 5.6 was written to distrust. EPRI and ENWL ship no time series, so
the question cannot be settled on them; it is stated as open in Section 7 and
left there.

This costs the paper's contribution nothing, because the contribution is a
decision procedure and not a number: Section 6.1 tells a utility which study
process to run, and that recommendation does not depend on any magnitude
surviving a year of weather.

One carve-out is genuine rather than defensive. The co-located and
oversubscribed cells (SMART-DS, Swiss) are reported for **dispatchable
generation under net-export control** — the "Energy Park" object the reform is
actually about, where a control mechanism holds the cancellation at all hours
by construction, so the coincidence those magnitudes depend on is enforced
rather than hoped for. We do not claim them for uncontrolled solar paired with
evening load, where Section 5.6 shows directly that the cancellation does not
hold.

Finally, a two-extreme-snapshot certification is a floor on this problem, not a
characterization of it: the Swiss full-profile result is markedly more fragile
than the SMART-DS corner check alone would have suggested. Wherever a real
profile exists, it should be run in full.

## 7. Limitations

**Temporal validity is measured on one feeder and untested on the rest.**
Section 6.2 scopes every magnitude in this paper as a snapshot magnitude. The
reason is not caution for its own sake: Ckt7, Ckt24 and ENWL residuals are the
same *kind* of claim (export cancelling draw on a shared constraint) as the
feeders Section 5.6 shows to be coincidence-bound, and PV is diurnally
near-zero for a large fraction of every real day regardless of feeder, so there
is no structural reason to expect their residuals are more temporally robust.
EPRI and ENWL ship no time series, so this cannot be tested on them, and we
neither apply an unmeasured discount to numbers we have not measured nor let
them stand as though the question did not apply. Closing this gap on a feeder
corpus that ships profiles is the single most valuable extension of this work.

**The invariant is not a blind predictor.** Section 5.2 reports this directly:
two mechanical operationalizations of "the active constraint admits both signs"
both fail on this corpus, and two feeders with identical binding patterns have
opposite residuals. The classification in Section 5.1's table is ours, made
knowing the residuals. What survives is a mechanism and two measured queue-side
predictors, not a rule readable off a baseline power flow.

**No number here is typical of distribution feeders in general.** Sections
5.3–5.4 are Ckt7-only. The ENWL residual fractions are large partly because
those feeders are PV-scarce (load-alone capacity ≈ 0), not because the effect
is typical. The corpus is broad enough to establish that the invariant governs
the sign and that the mechanism is real on four independent families; it is not
a representative sample of any utility's territory.

**Queue sizes are small.** Core cells are K=17 (Ckt7) and K=24–40 (Ckt24) at
100 kW; sweeps push Ckt24 to K=100. Enough to show the effect, its inversion,
and the thermal-to-voltage crossover; not enough to publish an absolute
capacity magnitude as representative.

**GLDF validity and AC-reverification completeness.** The
Generalized-LinDistFlow instrument (Section 4.1) is a linear approximation, not
a convex relaxation — its feasible set is not provably a superset of the true
AC-feasible one, so its own optimum before AC-reverification is an estimate
rather than a certified bound. Every number reported here is the AC-reverified
allocation. "AC-verified" means one converged power flow on the reported
accept-set: it confirms feasibility of that specific subset, not optimality.

**Thermal and voltage limit defaults.** EN 50160-loose LV limits (0.94–1.10
p.u., 100% loading) are used by default; correctness for the North American
feeders, held to a different regulatory standard, is a modeling choice. It was
checked for sensitivity (Section 5.7) but not independently verified against
each jurisdiction's actual tariff or planning rules.

**Request modeling.** Requests are constant-power injections at a single
representative snapshot (peak load, except the Swiss feeder's full profile); no
inverter Volt/VAr except the one directly-tested cell; no storage co-request,
though a large share of real PV requests in published interconnection-queue
data also request storage.

**EPRI baseline adaptations.** Both Ckt24 and Ckt7 carry pre-existing overloads
on their own declared load (an EPRI allocation-factor-vs-nameplate artifact),
handled by excluding affected buses from the candidate pool and affected
branches from the feasibility check — a modeling judgment worth an
electrical-engineering reviewer's attention.

These bound what magnitude claims this paper is entitled to make. They do not
bear on the central claim, that the residual's existence and sign are governed
by the both-aggressors invariant, which is robust to the exact limit values
used.

## 8. Conclusion

Segregating generation and load into separate interconnection study tracks
costs distribution hosting capacity on every feeder we tested — the parallel
gap is large everywhere — but that cost is only irreducible, surviving every
sequential study order, when the feeder's active physical constraint makes
generation and load complements: a two-sided voltage band almost always does, a
one-sided thermal or ampacity rating only where the binding element carries
reversible flow. That account holds across four independently-sourced feeder
families and two power-flow engines, and the regime crosses within a single
feeder as its queue's scale and granularity change. It is an explanation rather
than a test, though: when we tried to evaluate it blind, from single-signed
studies alone, two feeders with identical binding patterns turned out to have
opposite residuals. The properties that do predict the residual in advance are
the queue's, not the network's — oversubscription gates it, granularity sizes
it.

Where the residual depends on generation cancelling load at one moment in time,
we show directly — on the one feeder with the data to test it fully — that it
does not survive an ordinary day, and we scope the paper's magnitude claims
accordingly rather than assume the concern away.

The result a utility can act on today is therefore not a capacity number, and
not a reading of the baseline power flow, but a conditional about the queue in
front of it: co-study earns its cost on oversubscribed, lumpy queues, and can
be skipped at little cost on queues that fit or that are finely divided.
Whether it is worth paying for in a given case is decidable before the joint
study; how much it will return is not.

## Appendix A: the arrival-order lottery

The residual is what no study *order* recovers once the study boundary is
fixed. A separate loss lives inside a fixed boundary: which requests connect,
and how much capacity is realized, depend on the order in which a
first-come-first-served process happens to receive them. The two are different
interventions — re-ordering a queue versus co-studying it — and a utility pays
both, so this is reported here rather than competing with the paper's subject.

**Equal sizes buy capacity-optimality and nothing else.** A 50 kW monodisperse
Ckt7 queue shows capacity spread 1.00× across all sampled arrival orders —
exactly the uniform-matroid prediction of Section 3.3 — even while 19–98
individual requests remain blocked with positive seat-rate entropy. Order does
not change how much connects; it fully determines *who* does. Fairness and
capacity are separate questions on such a queue, and only the second one is
protected.

**Size inequality both creates the lottery and destroys capacity.** At fixed
total requested capacity, fixed mean request size and fixed count, inequality
of request sizes (coefficient of variation) both creates the capacity lottery
(Spearman +0.753; CV 2.0 → 4.05× spread) and destroys realized capacity (a 41%
loss, 916 → 538 kW). This is the one experimental design orthogonal to all
three scalars that a queue's `total = size × count` identity otherwise
confounds. Consistently, the size effect on spread is null on average (Spearman
≈ 0) but peaks at intermediate request sizes: tiny requests all fit regardless
of order, huge requests are seated a fixed number at a time regardless of
order, and the lottery lives only where the knapsack actually bites.

**What re-ordering alone is worth.** Using CA Rule 21 empirical request sizes
against 200 arrival orders (Ckt7, 6 scenarios), a joint search gains +14.9% to
+45.4% over the median arrival order, +2.9% to +22.3% over a p95 (lucky) order,
and the median order already attains 68.8–87.1% of achievable. Joint allocation
is worth tens of percent, not multiples: it insures against a bad draw rather
than unlocking a hidden multiple. That range is the same order of magnitude as
the 17%-of-joint median residual of Section 5.1, and the two are independent
and compounding.
